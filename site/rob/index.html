Challenge 1 of 4 · address poisoning

Can you spot a fake wallet address?

You paid Alex yesterday. A minute later a stranger sent you a fraction of a cent from an address that starts and ends like Alex's. Most wallets shorten addresses to their first and last few characters, so in your history the two look the same. Next time you copy "Alex" from your history, you might copy the thief.

Your call

Alex is in your contacts. You're paying Alex again, with the address you copied from your history. Same address, or a fake?

Kailah compares every address you enter with the ones you trust: your contacts, the addresses you've paid and your own. It warns when three characters match at both ends, or when one end alone matches as far as a fake can cheaply copy. Now play the thief: try each kind of fake on the form below.

    Challenge 2 of 4 · a node that rewrites your payment

    Can you detect a manipulated transaction?

    On TRON, your wallet asks a network node to build the payment, then signs the bytes that come back. The node also sends a readable summary of those bytes. A hacked or dishonest node can make the summary say one thing and the bytes another, and a wallet that checks only the summary signs what the bytes say.

    Your call

    You asked to send 250 TRX to Alex, and the node's summary says exactly that. Below are the fields inside the bytes you would sign. Sign them, or refuse?

    Kailah doesn't go by the summary. It decodes every byte it is about to sign, refuses any field it doesn't know or finds twice, and checks that the payment is exactly the one you asked for. Now play the node: pick an attack.

      Challenge 3 of 4 · a data service that lies about your coins

      Can you identify a suspicious network response?

      A classic Dogecoin signature doesn't cover how much the coin being spent is worth. So the wallet has to know each coin's amount, and it asks a data service. If the service says your 2,500 DOGE coin holds 25, the wallet builds a payment that hands the difference to the miners as a fee.

      Your call

      Your coin holds 2,500 DOGE. To spend it, the wallet asks a data service for the transaction that created the coin, and this comes back. Trust it?

      Kailah also fetches the transaction that created the coin, and keeps it only if its bytes hash to the coin's ID. Nobody can make different bytes with the same hash. If the service lies in its list of coins instead, the verified bytes still win: before anything goes out, Kailah checks that the fee the signed transaction really pays is the fee it planned.       Challenge 4 of 4 · the retry that pays twice

      Can you prevent a double payment?

      You send 100 USDC on Base, and the network doesn't answer. Maybe it got there, maybe not.

      Your call

      Your 100 USDC payment got no answer. What do you do?

      If your wallet signs a brand-new payment and the first one did get through, you've paid twice. Kailah signs the retry with the same sequence number, the nonce, at a 10% higher fee. The network accepts one payment per nonce, so whichever lands, the other never can.

        A wallet that signs a new payment

          Kailah

            And before any new send, Kailah checks whether the last one is still out there. If it might be, the form says so in the wallet's own words: "A new send now could pay twice. Wait for it, or replace it: the replacement takes its place at a higher fee, so only one of the two can ever go through."

            Every network gets its own version. On Solana and TRON a retry sends the same signed bytes again, and new sends wait until those bytes expire. Bitcoin, Dogecoin and Litecoin hold new sends for up to 30 minutes while an earlier attempt could still land.

              And there's more

              Twelve more things Kailah checks

              • Swaps can't hide a drain

                A swap service builds the swap. Kailah reads every instruction, allows only the programs a swap needs, pins where tokens leave and arrive to your own accounts, and refuses any extra transfer.

              • A Ledger can't lie

                Only signatures come back from the device. Each one is checked against Kailah's own record of the payment and fee you reviewed.

              • Practice can't sign for real

                Test networks use different keys, so a lying test node can't get a signature from you that works on the real network.

              • Only you make an address trusted

                Trust comes from payments you made and contacts you saved. A row in your history never counts, because a data service could fake one.

              • A capital-letter trap

                Written in capitals, three in ten Litecoin segwit addresses also read as a valid Solana address that nobody controls. Kailah's Solana form refuses them.

              • Speed-ups prove it's yours

                Before speeding up a stuck payment, Kailah checks that the pending transaction carries your wallet's own signatures.

              • Two servers must agree

                Before deciding an earlier Solana payment never happened, Kailah asks both of its servers. One server alone can't end the hold.

              • The code is never cached

                The offline helper stores only the offline screen. The wallet itself always loads fresh.

              • A fixed list of hosts

                The browser lets the app talk only to a fixed list of blockchain and price services. A test proves the list has nothing missing and nothing extra.

              • Money is whole numbers

                Every amount is counted as an integer in the coin's smallest unit. No floating point touches a balance.

              • Locked by default

                Your recovery phrase is encrypted with AES-256-GCM under a key derived with Argon2id, and the wallet locks after 15 minutes idle.

              • Anyone can check the build

                Rebuild the extension from the public source and you get the same bytes. After every release, the copy Google serves is checked against that build, file for file.
